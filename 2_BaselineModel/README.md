## Baseline Model Results

### Model Selection
- **Baseline Model Type:** Random Forest Regressor
- **Rationale:** Because Random Forest Regressor is most suited for our agricultural yield prediction challenge, it was chosen as the baseline model. First, Random Forest inherently reflects the complicated non-linear correlations found in crop yield data (the impacts of temperature, rainfall, and soil conditions on yield are not consistent across their ranges) without the need for manual parameterization. Second, interpretability is crucial for policy recommendations; Random Forest offers feature importance measures that pinpoint the variables (temperature, rainfall, and NDVI) that have the most effects on yields, providing agricultural extension services with useful information. Third, our dataset contains continuous variables (temperature, NDVI), categorical variables (region, crop type), and ordinal variables (disease severity). The model can handle mixed data types reliably without requiring a lot of preprocessing. Fourth, Random Forest's ensemble mechanism lowers variance and cross-validation verifies consistent generalization (R2 = 0.66 ± 0.04), making it a perfect standard for comparing more complicated models without overfitting. Lastly, it is the right baseline for both predictive benchmarking and causal inference because of its strong predictive performance (R² = 0.68, RMSE = 1.12 tons/ha), which guarantees accurate nuisance model estimate for the Double/Debiased Machine Learning framework.

### Model Performance
The baseline Random Forest model achieved the following performance scores: an R² Score of 0.68, indicating that 68% of the variance in crop yield is explained by the model; an RMSE of 1.12 tons/ha, meaning the typical prediction error is approximately ±1.12 tons per hectare; an MAE of 0.89 tons/ha, reflecting that half of the predictions fall within 0.89 tons/ha of actual values; and an MSE of 1.25 tons²/ha², confirming reasonably low squared prediction errors. The cross-validation R² of 0.66 ± 0.04 demonstrates stable performance across different data subsets, indicating good generalization.

### Evaluation Methodology
The model was evaluated using an 80/20 train-validation split, with 80% of the data (400 farms) used for training and 20% (100 farms) reserved for validation. This split ensures sufficient data for model fitting while retaining a representative holdout sample for unbiased performance assessment. Performance was assessed using regression metrics appropriate for continuous yield prediction: R² to measure the proportion of variance explained, RMSE to penalize large errors in the same units as the target variable (tons/ha), MAE for a robust average error estimate, and MSE for loss optimization. Additionally, 5-fold cross-validation was employed to evaluate model stability and generalization, yielding a mean R² of 0.66 ± 0.04. This methodology ensures reliable performance assessment while preventing overfitting, and the cross-validation results confirm consistent predictive power across different farm subsets.

### Metric Practical Relevance
The evaluation metrics chosen for this agricultural yield prediction project directly translate to real-world decision-making for farmers, policymakers, and agricultural extension services. The R² Score of 0.68 indicates that 68% of yield variation is explained by the model, which has significant practical implications: policymakers can confidently use the model to identify regional yield drivers and allocate resources to the most influential factors (such as irrigation improvements or soil management). However, the remaining 32% of unexplained variance highlights the need for complementary on-the-ground data collection, such as daily weather patterns and pest incidence, to achieve precision agriculture goals.
The RMSE of 1.12 tons/ha and MAE of 0.89 tons/ha provide directly interpretable error bounds for yield forecasts. At an average farm size of 12.8 hectares and a market price of $500 per ton, the RMSE translates to a prediction uncertainty of approximately **$7,168 per farm** ($500 × 1.12 tons × 12.8 ha). This level of uncertainty is acceptable for regional planning and risk assessment—for example, identifying farms at risk of yields below 4 tons/ha (potential intervention targets) or farms exceeding 7 tons/ha (model farms for best practices). For agricultural insurance companies, these error bounds help set premium rates and estimate payout risks, while for government subsidy programs, they ensure that resources are directed to farms with the highest probability of responding to interventions.
The MSE of 1.25 tons²/ha² serves primarily as an internal optimization metric, ensuring that the model prioritizes accurate predictions over extreme outliers. In practice, this means the model is less sensitive to anomalous yield values, which might occur due to measurement errors or extreme weather events, making predictions more reliable for typical farming conditions. The cross-validation R² of 0.66 ± 0.04 provides confidence that the model's performance is stable across different farm subsets, which is crucial for scaling the model to new regions or future growing seasons. Policymakers can trust that the model will maintain its predictive accuracy when applied to similar agricultural contexts, enabling consistent and equitable resource allocation across diverse farming communities.
Ultimately, these metrics collectively ensure that the model is "good enough" for policy targeting but not yet "precise enough" for individual farm prescriptions. The 68% explained variance and 1.12 tons/ha error mean that the model can successfully identify high-priority farms for intervention (those with predicted yields below 3 tons/ha) and benchmark high-performing farms (those above 8 tons/ha). For sustainable agriculture, this translates to targeted investments in drip irrigation and other practices, ensuring that limited public resources are directed to farms with the highest potential for yield improvement while avoiding wasted expenditures on farms where interventions are unlikely to succeed.
